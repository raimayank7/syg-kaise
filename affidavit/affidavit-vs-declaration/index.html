Affidavit, declaration and self-declaration compared

Offices ask for an affidavit, a declaration or a "self-declaration" without saying how they differ. Here is what the Acts tell us, and what they do not.

Sample, not legal advice. This page explains what the Act says in plain words and offers a general sample. It is not advice for your case. Offices, courts, registrars and notaries may ask for their own wording, forms or stamp paper, and the law of your state may add rules. Ask the office concerned or a lawyer before you sign.

Sources

Sources read on 13 November 2026: the Oaths Act, 1969 (India Code copy, "As on the 6th Oct, 2025"); the Notaries Act, 1952 (India Code copy).

What the law says about an affidavit

An affidavit carries an oath or affirmation. Form No. 4 in the Schedule to the Oaths Act, 1969 contains the verification, and section 3(2) lets persons empowered by the High Court or the State Government administer oaths and affirmations for affidavits. A notary may administer the oath or take the affidavit under section 8(1)(e) of the Notaries Act, 1952.

What the Acts we read do not say

The words "declaration" and "self-declaration" are not defined in the sections we read. A self-declaration is usually a plain signed statement with no oath. Whether an office treats it as enough is the office's rule, not something these Acts decide.

A practical way to choose

  • If the office says "affidavit", get an oath or affirmation taken by someone who may take it.
  • If it says "declaration" or "self-declaration", ask whether notarisation is needed.
  • If it says "attested", ask who may attest.
  • Write the office's name on your copy of the instruction so you can show what you were asked for.

Why it matters

Section 8 of the Oaths Act says every person giving evidence before a court or authorised person is bound to state the truth. A statement made under oath is taken more seriously than an unsworn note. Do not sign any statement whose facts you cannot stand behind.
