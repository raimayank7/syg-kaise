What is an affidavit?

An affidavit is a written statement of facts that the person making it swears or affirms to be true. This guide explains it from the text of the Oaths Act, 1969.

Sample, not legal advice. This page explains what the Act says in plain words and offers a general sample. It is not advice for your case. Offices, courts, registrars and notaries may ask for their own wording, forms or stamp paper, and the law of your state may add rules. Ask the office concerned or a lawyer before you sign.

Sources

Sources read on 13 November 2026: the Oaths Act, 1969 (India Code copy, "As on the 6th Oct, 2025"); the Notaries Act, 1952 (India Code copy); the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908, Order XIX (India Code copy).

What the law calls it

The Oaths Act, 1969 does not define the word "affidavit" in a section of its own, but it uses it in two places. Section 3(2) says that a court, Judge, Magistrate or person may administer oaths and affirmations "for the purpose of affidavits" if empowered to do so. The Schedule gives Form No. 4, headed "Affidavits": "I do swear in the name of God / solemnly affirm that this is my name and signature (or mark) and that the contents of this my affidavit are true."

The parts of an affidavit

An affidavit is made by a person called the deponent. A typical affidavit has a heading, the deponent's name, parentage, age and address, numbered paragraphs of facts, and a closing verification, which is the sworn or affirmed statement. The Form No. 4 wording above is the verification that the Act itself sets out. Our samples end with that wording.

Facts, not opinions

For court cases, Order XIX Rule 3(1) of the Code of Civil Procedure says affidavits "shall be confined to such facts as the deponent is able of his own knowledge to prove", except on interlocutory applications, where statements of belief may be admitted if the grounds are stated. Rule 3(2) adds that costs of an affidavit that unnecessarily sets out hearsay or argument, or copies of or extracts from documents, are to be paid by the party filing it, unless the court directs otherwise. Even outside court, writing only what you know yourself is the safe habit.

Who takes the oath

Section 3(2) of the Oaths Act lets certain persons administer oaths for affidavits if the High Court (for judicial proceedings) or the State Government (for other affidavits) has empowered them. The Notaries Act, 1952, section 8(1)(e), separately lists among a notary's functions to "administer oath to, or take affidavit from, any person". Read our guide on who can take an affidavit.

Common questions

Is an affidavit the same as a declaration?

The Oaths Act speaks only of oaths and affirmations. A document called a declaration may not carry any oath. See our comparison page, and ask the office what it accepts.

Does an affidavit need stamp paper?

This site states no stamp values because they differ by state. Section 35 of the Indian Stamp Act, 1899 says an instrument chargeable with duty cannot be admitted in evidence, acted upon, registered or authenticated unless duly stamped. Ask the notary or the office whether your affidavit is chargeable in your state.
