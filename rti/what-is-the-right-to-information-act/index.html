What the Right to Information Act, 2005 says

The Right to Information Act, 2005 lets citizens ask government bodies for records and get an answer in a set time. This page says who can ask and what you can ask for.

Sample, not legal advice. This page explains what the Act says in plain words and offers a general sample. It is not advice for your case. Offices, courts, registrars and notaries may ask for their own wording, forms or stamp paper, and the law of your state may add rules. Ask the office concerned or a lawyer before you sign.

Sources

Sources read on 13 November 2026: the Right to Information Act, 2005 (Gazette of India, Extraordinary, 21 June 2005, the copy kept at rti.gov.in, read through an Internet Archive snapshot of 2 April 2025) and the Right to Information Rules, 2012 (G.S.R. 603(E), 31 July 2012, the copy on cic.gov.in). We read the Act as originally published and the Rules of 2012. Later amendments of the Act (other than section 8(1)(j), see our page on personal information) and any amending rules were not read, so check the current text on the India Code or the Department of Personnel and Training site before you rely on a figure.

The right itself: section 3

Section 3 says: “Subject to the provisions of this Act, all citizens shall have the right to information.” The Act is therefore a right of citizens, and every other section sets out how that right is used or limited.

What counts as information: section 2(f)

Section 2(f) says information means any material in any form, including records, documents, memos, e-mails, opinions, advices, press releases, circulars, orders, logbooks, contracts, reports, papers, samples, models, data material held in any electronic form, and information relating to any private body which can be accessed by a public authority under any other law for the time being in force.

What the right includes: section 2(j)

Section 2(j) says the right to information means the right to information accessible under the Act which is held by or under the control of any public authority, and includes the right to inspection of work, documents and records; taking notes, extracts or certified copies; taking certified samples of material; and obtaining information in the form of diskettes, floppies, tapes, video cassettes or any other electronic mode or through printouts where the information is stored in a computer or other device.

Whom you can ask: section 2(h)

A public authority, under section 2(h), means any authority, body or institution of self-government established or constituted by or under the Constitution, by any other law made by Parliament or by a State Legislature, or by notification or order of the appropriate Government. It includes any body owned, controlled or substantially financed, and any non-Government organisation substantially financed, directly or indirectly, by funds provided by the appropriate Government. A private shop or a purely private company is not on that list; ask whether the body you have in mind fits one of these descriptions.

Each office has an officer for it: section 5

Section 5(1) tells every public authority to designate Central or State Public Information Officers in its administrative units or offices to provide information, and section 5(2) tells it to designate an Assistant Public Information Officer at each sub-divisional or sub-district level to receive applications and appeals and forward them. You can therefore hand your application to the Assistant officer if the main office is far away.

What the Act does not give you

The right is subject to the exemptions of sections 8 and 9, set out on our page on exemptions. A request can also be turned down for those reasons only, and the officer must say so in writing, as section 7(8) requires.

Common questions

Do I have to give a reason for asking?

No. Section 6(2) says an applicant shall not be required to give any reason for requesting the information or any other personal details except those necessary for contacting him or her.

Can a company or a non-citizen ask?

Section 3 gives the right to citizens. We have not read any other provision that widens it, so we do not say more; a lawyer can advise on a company or foreign national.
