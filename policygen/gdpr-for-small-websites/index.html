GDPR for small websites

The General Data Protection Regulation is the European Union's privacy law. This guide is built from its official text, read on 13 November 2026.

Source

Source read on 13 November 2026: Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR), official text on EUR-Lex (Articles 3, 6, 13 and Chapter III).

The UK has its own version, the UK GDPR, which this page does not describe because we did not read it; see the UK Information Commissioner's Office website.

When it applies to you

Article 3(2) says the Regulation applies to the processing of personal data of people who are in the Union by a controller not established in the Union, where the processing relates to the offering of goods or services to those people (whether or not payment is required), or to the monitoring of their behaviour in the Union. A site that ships to Europe, is aimed at Europeans, or tracks visitors there with analytics or advertising cookies may fall within that wording. If you are unsure, ask a lawyer.

A reason for every use of data

Article 6(1) says processing is lawful only if at least one of six bases applies: consent for specific purposes, necessity for a contract, a legal obligation, protecting vital interests, a task in the public interest, or the controller's legitimate interests (unless overridden by the person's rights). Small sites mostly rely on consent, contract (delivering an order) or legitimate interests.

What to tell people

Article 13(1) lists what you must tell people when you collect their data from them: your identity and contact details; the contact details of the data protection officer where there is one; the purposes and legal basis; the legitimate interests, if you rely on them; the recipients or categories of recipients; and any transfer to a country outside the Union with the safeguards used. Article 13(2) adds further items. The privacy policy generator covers these in plain words, but you must fill in your own details and check the rest.

Rights of people (Chapter III)

The Regulation names these rights:

  • Right of access (Article 15)
  • Right to rectification (Article 16)
  • Right to erasure (Article 17)
  • Right to restriction of processing (Article 18)
  • Right to data portability (Article 20)
  • Right to object (Article 21)

A short to-do list

  • Publish a privacy policy that matches reality (use our generator and pick EU/UK).
  • Choose and write down your legal basis for each use of data.
  • Ask for consent before non-essential cookies for visitors in Europe (see the AdSense page for Google's requirement on ad sites).
  • Decide who answers requests from people, and how fast.
  • Keep a simple list of what personal data you hold and where.

Common questions

Does the GDPR apply to an Indian blog?

Only if Article 3 applies as described above. Many sites with ads follow it for visitors from Europe anyway.

Do I need a data protection officer?

This page does not cover when one is required. Read the Regulation or ask a lawyer.
