Printable chore chart

Type the family members' names, separated by commas; each gets a row for the seven days.

Making a chart that works

Chores stick when everybody knows what is theirs and can see it done. Write each person's tasks in their row for the day, or write the chore initials and tick when done. The chart redraws as you type names, up to a page of rows.

Tips for families

  • Share the work fairly and by age. Small children can set the table or water plants.
  • Rotate the unpopular chores weekly.
  • Agree the chart together; people keep promises they helped to write.
  • Praise effort, and keep any reward simple.

Offices and shops

The same chart works for cleaning duty, opening and closing, or stock checks in a small business. Replace names with roles and print a fresh copy each week.

Starting a chart with children

Sit down together and list every household job. Let each child choose one job they like and one they do not. Write the jobs on the chart with the child's name and tick each day. Review after a week and change anything that did not work. A chart works when it is fair and visible; children are more willing to follow rules that they helped to write.

Ideas for adapting it

Keep jobs specific: "empty the dustbin" beats "help at home". Move names down one row each week so that nobody has the same job for ever. For young children, draw a small picture beside each job. Put the chart at child height and let them tick it themselves.

Printing this page

The page opens as A4 landscape with a 10 mm margin. Change any of these in the form: A3 gives more writing room, A5 fits a folder, Letter suits North American printers. Press Print and choose Actual size so the sheet matches the preview proportions, or choose Save as PDF to keep it. Print a single test page first; check that nothing is cut at the edge before you print a pack.

Common questions

How many people fit?

Up to about ten rows stay comfortable on A4 landscape.
