Choosing graph paper grid spacing

A grid that is too fine strains the eyes; one that is too coarse loses detail. Match the grid to the task.

Guide by task

  • Schoolwork and general graphs: 5 mm.
  • Young children and posters: 10 mm.
  • Engineering sketches and calibration: 1 or 2 mm, light line.
  • Floor plans: 6.35 mm (quarter inch) or 5 mm, one square for a foot or half a metre.
  • Bullet journals and notes: 5 mm dot grid.
  • Pixel art and cross-stitch: 2.5 to 4 mm squares with bold lines every 10.

Pick the scale first

Before choosing paper, decide what one square stands for. If your data runs from 0 to 100 and you want the graph to fill the page, divide the number of squares along the axis by 100. A 5 mm grid on A4 gives about 50 squares along the shorter side, so 1 square = 2 units fits well.

Lines and colours

Use a light line so the data stands out. Pale blue, grey or green is easier on the eyes than black and photocopies better than very light yellow.

A worked example

Suppose you measure the temperature of water every minute for 20 minutes, from 20 to 90 degrees. On A4 portrait with a 10 mm margin there are 38 squares across and 55 down at 5 mm. Put time on the shorter side: 20 minutes over 38 squares means one minute is about 1.9 squares, so choose 2 squares per minute, which uses 40 squares and fits only if you reduce the margin, or choose 1 square per minute and use half the width. Put temperature on the long side: 70 degrees over 55 squares, so 1 square can stand for 1.5 degrees, but round to 1 square = 2 degrees for easy reading. Choosing round scales is what makes a graph readable, and the paper only has to be big enough to hold them.

Common questions

Should the major lines be every 5 or every 10?

Every 5 for 5 mm grids; every 10 for 1 or 2 mm grids.
